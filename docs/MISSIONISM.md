# Missionism

**A system for people to build, fund, and own long-term missions together.**

**Public one-pager:** [`BUILD_THE_NEXT_ERA.md`](./BUILD_THE_NEXT_ERA.md) — *Build the next era. Own part of what you create.*

Site: `/system`. Constitution: `spec/CONSTITUTION.md`. Certification: `spec/CERTIFICATION.md`. Mishys: `spec/MISHYS.md`. Spec pointers: `spec/OWNERSHIP.md`, `spec/ORGANIZATION.md`, `spec/MCU_PROTOCOL.md`. Ownership architecture: `OWNERSHIP_AND_CAPITAL_SYSTEM.md`.

---

## The core idea (short)

Most startups grow by selling pieces of the company to investors.

Missionism offers another path:

> **Use ownership to reward the humans who create the next round of value.**

Normal companies, shares, salaries, independent valuations, profits, boards, debt, contracts.

What changes: an **Era** unlocks contributor ownership only when **Mission · Financial · Integrity** gates all advance. Existing owners keep most of the new pie. Contributors earn a published share of the increment. Next generation can always mine tomorrow — never yesterday.

Central bargain: **Create $5. Keep $1. Make everyone richer.**

Full public narrative: [BUILD_THE_NEXT_ERA.md](./BUILD_THE_NEXT_ERA.md).

## The longer story

Missionism starts with one belief:

> People can do more together when they share a clear mission, know what winning means, have freedom to act, and can earn ownership in what they build.

Today, capitalism, philanthropy, religion, and crowdfunding each solve part of this problem.

| Piece | What it is already good at |
|-------|----------------------------|
| **Capitalism** | Creating value and compounding money |
| **Philanthropy** | Aiming money at human problems |
| **Religion** | Meaning, identity, and long time horizons |
| **Kickstarter** | Helping people organize around something they want to exist |
| **Slicing Pie** | Helping people earn pieces of something as they contribute and take risk |
| **Bitcoin** | A system can begin with one creator and slowly become independent of that creator if the rules are set ahead of time and become hard to change |

Missionism tries to combine those ideas.

Pick a mission. Agree on what progress means. Let people organize around it. Let them create value in any useful way. Let contributors earn ownership. Slowly move the founder’s ownership to the people building the mission. Make the rules harder to change than the people running the system.

---

## 1. Start with a Mission

A Mission describes a future people want to help create.

Example: **Increase the global supply of excellent healthcare workers.**

A Mission can last 20, 50, or 100 years. That is different from a company goal. A company might disappear. A product might fail. A CEO might leave. The Mission can continue.

**Companies are tools of the Mission. The Mission is the thing meant to last.**

---

## 2. Define what better means

Every Mission gets a Value Equation. The Value Equation turns a vague idea like “improve healthcare” into something people can actually work on.

Example shape:

```
more excellent healthcare workers × better access × faster readiness × stronger retention
= more Mission progress
```

The exact equation is different for every Mission. Its job is to answer: **If we made this number better, would the world actually be closer to the Mission?**

The equation is not perfect truth. It is the best current map.

---

## 3. The Mission is harder to change than the map

The closer something is to the Mission, the harder it should be to change. This gives long-term stability without making everyday work slow.

| Change friction | What |
|-----------------|------|
| **Very hard** | The Mission · basic human protections · founder equity transition rules · rights people already earned |
| **Hard** | The Value Equation · the equity mining schedule |
| **Easier** | 5-year goals · sub-missions · company strategy |
| **Easy** | Products · teams · prices · managers · experiments |

This is the same direction as Global G12 (Evolution): change friction increases upward.

---

## 4. Break big Missions into smaller Missions

A 100-year Mission is too large to attack directly. So it splits.

```
Improve human health
  → Increase excellent healthcare workers
      → Make healthcare competency measurable
          → Build Benchmark

Improve human health
  → Reduce cardiovascular deaths
      → Improve hypertension control
          → Build a different project or company
```

Each smaller Mission can have its own people, goals, projects, companies, funding, Value Equation, and equity pie. Big Missions become trees of smaller Missions people can actually work on.

---

## 5. The founder starts with the company

The founder goes first. They take the early risk, organize the first people, create the first version. So the founder can begin with most or all of the company.

The major difference from normal capitalism: **the founder agrees at the beginning that their original ownership is temporary.**

A **Founder Clock** starts. Maybe 20 years. Maybe 30. The larger Mission might last 50 or 100 years. By the end of the Founder Clock, up to 100% of the founder’s original ownership can be earned by the people who helped build the Mission.

The founder should not be able to cancel this later because the company became valuable.

**Assumption:** it is easier to commit this when the Mission is worth nothing than later, when it is worth something. Bake it in at the start.

---

## 6. Time makes founder equity available

Every year, another part of the founder’s ownership becomes **available to be mined**. The schedule is written down in advance. It might be slow at first and faster later.

Example only (not a constitution):

| Year | Up to available |
|------|-----------------|
| 5 | 10% |
| 10 | 25% |
| 15 | 45% |
| 20 | 70% |
| 25 | 90% |
| 30 | 100% |

The important part: **the clock is known before anyone knows how valuable the company will become.** Future Founder cannot say “I changed my mind.”

Availability is not automatic transfer. See §7.

---

## 7. Mission progress unlocks more ownership

Time alone should not move all the equity. The Mission must also move.

A Mission can have 5-year goals tied to the Value Equation. Example: help 1 million additional healthcare workers reach a verified competency standard. If that goal is hit, another founder slice may unlock.

Two types of mining:

| Kind | What it rewards |
|------|-----------------|
| **Time Mining** | Small, steady amounts for keeping a strong institution alive and growing over many years |
| **Mission Mining** | Larger amounts for real progress toward the Mission |

The simplest rule:

> Time makes equity available. Mission progress unlocks it. Contribution earns it.

This is how the Founder Clock and the earlier “not a calendar giveaway” rule fit together. A calendar does not hand over ownership. It opens a pool. Progress opens more of that pool. Contribution decides who receives each slice.

---

## 8. Contribution decides who gets each slice

Suppose another 2% of founder equity gets unlocked. Now: who earned it?

Slicing-Pie-style rules help. People can earn slices through agreed contributions such as: work, skills, money, risk, compensation given up, important results, major projects, other agreed contributions.

The rules are known **before** the work happens. The system is not trying to decide how much a human being is worth. It is asking: **what did this person put into this pie under the rules we already agreed to?**

**Open design need:** extremely clear and transparent contribution rules, in the spirit of Slicing Pie. MCU accounting (`spec/MCU_PROTOCOL.md`) is the current mechanism, not the principle.

---

## 9. Workers still get normal compensation

Missionism should not require people to sacrifice their family’s safety. Workers get normal pay. They can also get equity.

A role might have a total pay value of $150,000. One worker might choose $130k cash / $20k equity; another $120k / $30k; another $110k / $40k.

There should be safe limits. Maybe no more than 20–30% of pay can be voluntarily moved into extra equity.

> Choosing cash never means you believe in the Mission less.

People have different families, ages, savings, and risk needs. Missionism should let them choose.

---

## 10. Earned equity stays yours

Once a person earns equity, it becomes their asset. If they quit, retire, get fired, change careers, or join another Mission, they keep what they already earned. They simply stop earning new slices.

People should stay because they choose the Mission, not because leaving destroys their wealth.

Over time, Mission Equity should become easier to hold, sell, trade under clear rules, borrow against, use for retirement, and move into other Mission investments. The long-term goal is to help ordinary workers become owners of assets, not only earn wages. See `OWNERSHIP_AND_CAPITAL_SYSTEM.md`.

---

## 11. Old ownership slowly dilutes

Future workers also need a chance to own what they build. As new slices are mined, old owners slowly dilute.

Someone might leave owning 1%. Twenty years later, after thousands of people build more value, they may own 0.4%. But the company may also be much more valuable. Their old work still created real wealth. They just do not own a fixed percentage of everything future workers create forever.

Each generation gets a chance to earn part of the future it builds.

---

## 12. The founder is racing the clock too

In normal capitalism, a founder can win by making the company more valuable **and** keeping as much ownership as possible. Missionism removes the second game. The founder already knows: my percentage is going down.

So the best strategy becomes: **make the pie much bigger.**

- 100% of $1 million = $1 million
- 50% of $1 billion = $500 million
- 10% of $100 billion = $10 billion
- 1% of $1 trillion = $10 billion

The founder can still become extremely wealthy. They become wealthy by making the Mission and company much larger, not by protecting ownership forever.

---

## 13. Leadership is not the same as ownership

The founder can lose ownership and still lead. Or own a lot and still be removed. These are different things:

| Layer | Question |
|-------|----------|
| **Ownership** | Who owns economic value? |
| **Leadership** | Who currently runs the company? |
| **Mission power** | Who can change the deepest rules? |

A founder might own 5% in Year 25 and still be CEO because everyone believes they are the best leader. Or own 80% in Year 8 and choose a better CEO because there are better people to lead the Mission.

Leadership should be earned continuously.

---

## 14. Money cannot buy the Mission

Economic ownership should not automatically buy Mission control.

Someone may own $100 million of equity. Someone else may own $20,000. That does not mean the first person gets 5,000 times more say over the Mission.

Missionism separates:

- **Economic ownership** — can have different values; can potentially be sold or borrowed against.
- **Mission citizenship** — constitutional voice; cannot simply be purchased.

> Money can buy economic exposure. Money cannot buy the Mission.

**Open:** how Mission citizenship should work needs more design. Treat as a problem to test, not a finished belief. See §27.

---

## 15. Use a Mission Board

A Mission Board protects the long-term agreement. It does **not** run the company. It should not decide product features, marketing, sales, hiring, prices, or software.

Its narrow job is to protect: the Mission, the Value Equation, the Founder Clock, the mining rules, and rights people already earned.

Think of it like a court or referee. Management plays the game. The Mission Board protects the rules.

(Related: Mission Council in the protocol constitution — protect Mission meaning; do not run strategy or award arbitrary MCUs.)

---

## 16. Nobody can rewrite yesterday

One rule should be extremely strong:

> You cannot change the deal after the work was already done.

If the rules said “do X and earn Y,” and someone does X, then Y belongs to them. Future rules can change future work. They cannot rewrite the past.

This protects workers from founders, boards, investors, and future majorities.

---

## 17. Profit is a tool, not the Mission

Missionism does not say every Mission activity must make money. A Mission may use profitable companies, nonprofit projects, donations, research, free services, prizes, investment funds, public programs, volunteers.

The Mission decides what is useful.

Example: a Mission to reduce malaria might find Project A costs $10 million and saves many lives but earns no money; Project B builds a profitable diagnostic company; Project C invests money now and may return far more in 20 years. All three may be useful.

The question is not “is this capitalism or philanthropy?” The question is: **which use of our money, time, people, and knowledge moves the Mission the most?**

---

## 18. Missionism changes philanthropy

Traditional philanthropy often works like: make money first → give some away later.

Missionism tries: create value and do good inside the same long-term system.

Sometimes the best Mission action will make money. Sometimes it will lose money. Sometimes it will give something away. Sometimes it will invest for decades.

Profit is useful because it lets the Mission keep funding itself. Profit is not the final score.

> The Mission is the score. Capital is a tool.

This is the same constitutional tension as `MISSION_ENTERPRISE_BRIDGE.md`: optimize the business to fund the Mission; optimize the Mission to decide what businesses should exist.

---

## 19. The Giving Pledge moves upstream

The Giving Pledge says: become wealthy, then promise to give much of the wealth away.

Missionism says: **before the wealth exists, commit the wealth-producing institution to the Mission and to the people who will build it.**

```
Traditional:  Founder gets rich → founder gives wealth away
Missionism:   Founder starts → people build → value grows → founder ownership gets mined
              → contributors become owners → Mission keeps compounding
```

The founder does not need to decide later how generous to be. The rules were created before the stakes became huge.

## 20. Long-term thinking is built into the system

| Clock | Horizon |
|-------|---------|
| Mission | 100 years |
| Founder Clock | 20–30 years |
| Major Mission goals | 5 years |
| Mining and operating goals | 1 year |
| Experiments | Weeks or months |

Think for 100 years. Experiment this week.

**[step by step, ferociously]**

## 21. People should be able to try different paths

People do not have to agree about the best way to reach a Mission. If two teams agree about the goal but disagree about the path, let them try both.

Example: two groups want to reduce heart disease. One wants better medicine. One wants better food and exercise. Both can work. Reality can help tell which approach works better.

Missionism should agree strongly on what better means and be much looser about how people get there.

People can also support several Missions with their unique skill sets and assets. A marketplace can form. See the Addendum.

---

## 22. Missions can form communities

A Mission can become bigger than one company. It can include companies, nonprofits, researchers, families, investors, volunteers, schools, projects, sub-missions.

People may spend their whole careers on one Mission. Families may care about one Mission for generations. But children should still be free to choose their own.

Missionism should create belonging without trapping people.

---

## 23. Mishys.com

Mishys can become the place where people find and organize around Missions.

Think: **Reddit + Discord + Kickstarter + GitHub + Slicing Pie**, organized around Missions.

A person could visit “Prevent Alzheimer’s · Year 12 of 100” and see: what winning means, the Value Equation, current progress, active goals, companies, projects, people, money committed, help needed, ways to work, invest, and earn slices.

Then they can join by offering time, skills, money, a company, an idea, research, an introduction, a new sub-mission.

The goal is to turn strangers who care about the same future into collaborators.

Mishys is the SAFE of Missionism: opinionated, never authoritative. If Mishys fails, the protocol survives.

---

## 24. The Bitcoin lesson

Bitcoin did not start with millions of people. It started with one creator. Then a few people joined. The important part was that the system had rules that could eventually work without the creator.

Missionism should do the same. The founder starts the Mission. The founder writes the first rules. Then the rules slowly make the founder unnecessary.

- Do not trust the founder to stay generous. Make the equity transition automatic.
- Do not trust future CEOs to protect the Mission. Make the Mission hard to change.
- Do not trust people to stay forever. Let them leave with what they earned.
- Do not trust one leader to live forever. Build something that can work without them.

> Believe in people. Do not depend on kings.

## 25. The flywheel

People find a Mission they care about → they join → they receive normal pay and can earn ownership → they build useful things → some projects make money → some projects spend money where it creates more Mission value → the Mission ecosystem gets stronger → the Value Equation improves → Mission progress unlocks founder equity → contributors mine that equity → workers become owners → people see that doing important work can also build personal wealth → more talented people join → more people create more knowledge, companies, capital, and Mission progress → more founder ownership gets mined → the founder becomes less important → the Mission becomes stronger → repeat across generations.

> Make helping yourself and helping the Mission point in the same direction as often as possible.

---

## 26. What Missionism believes

Missionism does not assume people are saints. People want money, purpose, family, safety, ownership, status, freedom, belonging, growth, meaning.

A good system should not require people to stop wanting those things. It should try to align them.

Build the system so doing well personally often means doing good for the Mission.

---

## 27. What we still need to solve

Missionism is a hypothesis. Treat these as design problems to test, not beliefs to defend:

- What is the best Founder Clock?
- How much equity should Time Mining unlock?
- How much should Mission Mining unlock?
- How do we measure contribution without creating bureaucracy?
- How do we prevent Value Equation gaming?
- How should Mission citizenship work?
- How should Mission Boards be chosen?
- How do we make the founder commitment legally hard to reverse?
- How should private Mission Equity be priced?
- How can workers sell or borrow against it?
- How do Missions handle conflicts with other Missions?
- How do we keep the system simple at 10 people and at 100,000?

## 28. Start with Benchmark

Benchmark can be the first real Missionism experiment.

| Layer | Test |
|-------|------|
| **Mission** | Increase the global supply of excellent healthcare workers |
| **Mission Clock** | Possibly 50–100 years |
| **Founder Clock** | Test something around 20–30 years |
| **Value Equation** | Define what real progress toward excellent healthcare-worker supply means |
| **Time Mining** | Each year, part of founder ownership becomes mineable |
| **Mission Mining** | Important verified Mission goals unlock additional ownership |
| **Contribution** | Simple Slicing-Pie-style rules for who earns each newly mined slice |
| **Compensation** | Normal market compensation with a safe, voluntary cash/equity choice |
| **Exit** | Earned equity stays with the worker |
| **Governance** | Rules even the founder cannot casually reverse |

Then measure: Do better people join? Do they trust the system? Do they choose equity? Do they stay longer? Do they share bad news faster? Do they take more ownership? Do they collaborate better? Does the company grow faster? Does the Mission move faster? Does the Founder Clock change founder behavior?

If it works, improve it. If it fails, learn why.

Until the activation loop works, do not ship lending, full capital markets, or governance theater. See `BUILD_PLAN.md`.

## The simplest explanation

A new person should understand Missionism in a few minutes:

- Here is the Mission.
- Here is how we measure progress.
- Here is the Founder Clock.
- Every year, more founder ownership can become available.
- Mission progress can unlock more.
- The people who contribute earn those slices.
- You still get normal pay.
- You can choose some cash or equity based on the risk that fits your life.
- Anything you earn stays yours if you leave.
- Money cannot buy the Mission.
- The founder cannot take the system back once the rules are set.
- Over time, the founder becomes just another person holding the slices they earned.

Our job is to build the biggest, most useful pie we can while completing the Mission together.

## Addendum — marketplace, not one company

Missionism is not just “join one mission and work there.” It is a marketplace of people, skills, capital, and missions.

A person can have their own personal mission and still contribute to many larger missions. A designer might care most about climate, but also do paid work for a healthcare mission and a local education mission. An engineer might split time across three sub-missions. A donor might fund ten. A company might provide tools to fifty.

```
People have skills and personal missions.
Missions have needs.
Mishys matches them.
Work creates value.
Value can be paid in cash, equity, or both.
```

That makes Missionism much more like an economic network than a company model.

### The marketplace layer

Every person could have a profile with: what I can do · what I care about · how I want to contribute · how I want to be paid (cash, equity, or a mix).

Every Mission posts what it needs now: logo design, regulatory help, clinical research, sales, software, fundraising, legal, manufacturing, a CEO, a volunteer.

A designer does not need to “join healthcare” forever. This month: identity system for the Alzheimer’s mission for $5,000 + contribution units. Next month: packaging for a clean-water company, cash only. Later: childhood literacy, more equity, because they care deeply.

That is personal agency over where skills go. Missions compete to attract contributors with some mix of purpose + cash + ownership + learning + relationships + future upside.

### This also fixes a weakness in the one-company model

If ownership only comes through traditional employment, most people can only meaningfully participate in one mission at a time. Humans don’t work that way. You can care about family, healthcare, climate, your city, AI safety, and education all at once.

Missionism should let your portfolio of work reflect your portfolio of values.

Instead of “What company do you work for?” the more Missionist question is: **What missions are you contributing to?**

### Specialization

You do not want every mission hiring its own mediocre lawyer, designer, recruiter, accountant, marketer, bank, or software team. Specialists can serve many missions.

A design studio could exist specifically to serve Missionist organizations. A bank could lend against Mission Equity across hundreds of missions. A recruiting firm could help missions find aligned talent. An audit firm could verify Value Equations. A Mission Board service could provide governance infrastructure. A software company could run contribution accounting.

Missions create demand. Specialists build expertise. Specialists serve many missions. Competition improves quality and price. That’s markets doing what markets are good at.

### Small slices across many pies

The designer does not need to become a giant owner in every organization. Maybe they earn 0.002% here, 0.01% there, cash somewhere else, and a much larger stake in the mission they dedicate five years to.

Over a career, a person could build a **portfolio of Mission Equity** from all the things they helped create. Retirement wealth might literally be a record of the useful things they helped build during their life. Because they can later diversify, sell, or potentially borrow against those assets, this becomes real household capital rather than symbolic points.

### Personal missions become another layer

```
PERSON          What do I want my life to contribute toward?
  → MISSIONS    Which futures do I care about?
  → SKILLS      What can I actually contribute?
  → MARKETPLACE Which missions currently need those skills?
  → PROJECT     What work can I do now?
  → COMPENSATION Cash / equity / mix
  → HISTORY     What did I help create?
  → WEALTH + IMPACT  What assets and mission progress resulted?
```

You don’t surrender yourself to a Mission. You direct your finite skills and time across Missions.

### Mishys as the coordination market

Mishys isn’t simply Reddit for causes. It becomes a marketplace where missions publish what they are trying to accomplish and people offer the skills, capital, and work needed to accomplish it.

| Layer | Job |
|-------|-----|
| Social / community | Discover each other |
| Kickstarter | Capital forms |
| Labor market | Work gets done |
| Slicing Pie | Track contribution |
| Equity | Contributors participate in upside |
| Value Equation | What progress actually means |

**Core principle:**

> People do not belong to Missions. Their contributions do.

That protects individual freedom and makes the system much more scalable. Someone can spend 60% of their career on one mission, 20% consulting across five others, 10% on family/community, 10% exploring something new. Missionism shouldn’t tell them that’s wrong. It should make it easy.

The simplest summary:

> Missionism creates a market where people can aim their unique skills at the missions they care about most—and earn pieces of the value they help create across all of them.
