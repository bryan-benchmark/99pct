# Ownership That Changes Your Life

**Why Missionism companies should be built to stay private, share ownership with contributors, and make that ownership useful.**

**Top sentence:**

> A paycheck pays for today. Ownership should help pay for tomorrow.

---

## 1. The simple idea

Most companies rent people's time. They pay wages for today's work. If the company becomes much more valuable, most employees do not own much of what they helped create.

Missionism changes that. People receive competitive pay for today's work and earn real ownership for helping create long-term value and move the mission forward.

The goal is not to replace salary with stock. The goal is to add a second engine of wealth.

```
SALARY  +  OWNERSHIP  +  USEFUL FINANCIAL TOOLS  =  MORE LIFETIME FREEDOM
```

---

## 2. What we are optimizing for

Missionism is not trying to maximize the stock price. It is not trying to maximize employee votes. It is not trying to give away the most equity possible.

It is trying to build companies that make extraordinary progress toward a mission for decades while helping the people who create that progress build real wealth.

```
Mission progress × long-term behavior × useful employee ownership × capital efficiency × resilience
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
short-term pressure × financial fragility × governance capture × complexity
```

---

## 3. Ownership starts with the founder, then moves toward contributors

A Missionism company can begin with a founder or mission leader owning nearly 100% of the company. That makes sense. At the beginning, the founder took the unusual risk, created the first system, and needs the freedom to move quickly.

But the company is born with a promise: **future value creates future ownership.**

Over decades, Contributor Shares are earned by the people who help move the mission forward. The founder is diluted as new ownership is created. The transition is gradual, measured in decades rather than quarters.

This is not a forced giveaway. The founder keeps the economic ownership already earned. Employees keep the economic ownership they earn. New contribution creates new ownership.

Governance also changes over time. Early on, the founder leads. As the contributor base becomes large and mature, governance moves toward the active contributors. **Wealth can be inherited. Permanent political control should not automatically be inherited.**

---

## 4. Equity mining: reward the right behavior

Equity mining is the engine that creates new Contributor Shares.

The system should reward the behaviors that make a mission durable: long-term thinking, small empowered teams, experiments, big bets when the evidence supports them, patient step-by-step execution, learning from failure, and real progress toward mission goals.

Missionism should not turn work into a points game.

Equity should be allocated at three levels:

| Level | Question |
|-------|----------|
| **Company** | How much durable value and mission progress did the whole organization create? |
| **Team** | Which small teams produced meaningful outcomes? |
| **Individual** | Who made unusually important contributions inside those teams? |

Mission-critical milestones can unlock larger pools. Normal years can create smaller pools. The formula should be predictable enough to trust, but not so mechanical that people learn to game it.

MCU accounting (`spec/MCU_PROTOCOL.md`) records contribution. Contributor Shares are the long-term economic mapping. Never hard-code `1 MCU = X shares`.

---

## 5. The ownership must be useful

Paper wealth is not enough.

An employee who owns $300,000 of a private company but cannot use it may not feel wealthy. Missionism should make ownership useful without constantly forcing people to sell it.

Employees should be able to do three simple things:

- **Hold** it and let it compound.
- **Sell** a reasonable portion during organized liquidity windows.
- **Use** a conservative portion as support for carefully designed financing.

The default should be to hold. The system should make it easier to remain an owner, not easier to spend tomorrow's wealth today.

---

## 6. The first big unlock: buying a home

Housing makes the idea tangible.

Example: Maya has worked at a Missionism company for five years. She earns a normal salary and has also accumulated $500,000 of vested Contributor Shares.

She wants to buy a home. Instead of telling Maya to sell $100,000 of her ownership for the down payment, the Missionism ecosystem helps her use that ownership as financial strength.

A simple version might use a conservative 5:1 collateral rule: roughly $5 of eligible share value for each $1 of supported financing. The exact rule would be set by professional credit analysis, not by ideology.

Maya gets a normal 30-year mortgage from a regulated mortgage lender. The system helps arrange down-payment financing, shows her the full monthly cost, and shows what her paycheck will look like after the purchase.

She can also get help from a salaried or flat-fee home advocate whose job is to help her make a good decision, not to maximize a sales commission.

**Five years of contribution → ownership → home-buying power**

The company did not give Maya a free house. Maya still pays her mortgage. But her work helped create the capital that allowed her to enter the housing market without necessarily selling the asset she spent years building.

---

## 7. A $10,000 Life Buffer

The smaller version may be just as important.

A vested employee could have access to a simple emergency buffer, perhaps up to $10,000 depending on income, ownership, and applicable lending rules.

The employee could repay it automatically over time through authorized payroll deductions or another normal payment method.

The goal is not to create a profitable lending business. The goal is to stop a broken air conditioner, medical bill, move, or car repair from forcing someone to put $6,000 on a high-interest credit card or sell a long-term asset.

Any zero-interest or subsidized loan program would need proper tax, lending, wage-deduction, and state-law design. Missionism should partner with regulated institutions first and only vertically integrate financial services when scale truly justifies it.

**Product note:** Lending against ownership is Phase 7 / explicitly not yet. See `BUILD_PLAN.md`. This document is doctrine, not a shipping checklist.

---

## 8. The same system can improve the rest of life

Once ownership becomes usable, Missionism can create an employee financial operating system.

| Need | How Missionism helps | Principle |
|------|----------------------|-----------|
| Home | Mortgage partners, down-payment support, home advocate | Use ownership to help acquire a durable asset |
| Emergency | Small low-cost buffer with simple repayment | Protect long-term ownership |
| Education | Low-cost financing or subsidy for valuable training | Invest in human capital |
| Car | Negotiated pricing and financing with clear paycheck impact | Be more conservative with depreciating assets |
| Insurance | Group purchasing and transparent comparison | Increase purchasing power |
| Relocation | Up-front capital repaid over time | Remove cash-flow friction |
| New mission | Potential financing against a small part of accumulated ownership | Let one mission seed another |
| Retirement | Liquidity, diversification, and income planning | Turn decades of ownership into durable freedom |

---

## 9. Why not just pay people more cash?

Cash is essential. Missionism employees should receive competitive salaries.

But one extra dollar of salary is spent once. One dollar of ownership can compound for decades.

The better question is not, "How can the company pay the highest salary?" It is:

> How can each dollar of compensation create the most lifetime security, wealth, agency, and mission progress?

A strong package can combine salary for today, bonuses for exceptional near-term work, Contributor Shares for long-term value creation, retirement benefits, and financial infrastructure that helps employees use their wealth intelligently.

---

## 10. Why not make everything public?

Public markets solve real problems extremely well. They provide continuous price discovery, deep liquidity, easier diversification, acquisition currency, and broad access to capital.

But they also create a second scoreboard that changes every second.

A company can make the right ten-year decision and watch its stock fall because interest rates changed, analysts expected more this quarter, or the entire market sold off. Employees who borrow against public stock can also face dangerous pressure if lenders mark collateral to market and demand more collateral after a sharp decline.

Missionism is trying to make the mission the highest-order scoreboard. Continuous public pricing can pull attention toward a different scoreboard: the stock price.

Public ownership is therefore useful, but it should not be the default destination.

---

## 11. Why private is our intended destination

A private Missionism company can use a slower clock.

Instead of a price every second, the company can publish a rigorous independent value periodically, such as quarterly. Employees can see whether their ownership is growing without turning every market movement into a company-wide emotional event.

Private ownership also gives the company more room to experiment, take long bets, protect unusual governance, and avoid spending organizational attention on analysts, quarterly narratives, and public-market headlines.

The trade-off is that Missionism must deliberately build some infrastructure that public markets normally provide.

---

## 12. What the private system must build

- Independent recurring valuation, supported by high-quality financial reporting.
- **Contributor Shares** that carry employee economics and governance.
- **Capital Shares** that outside investors can own without buying the mission.
- A one-way path from Contributor Shares to Capital Shares when employees want external liquidity.
- Regular liquidity windows rather than an unlimited promise that the company will always repurchase shares.
- Approved institutional buyers, employee buyers, and diversified Missionism investment funds.
- Standardized lending rules so banks can understand eligible private shares as collateral.
- Conservative credit products that do not create automatic margin calls when valuation falls.
- A path for employees to diversify as their wealth becomes concentrated.

The operating company should never promise unlimited liquidity. In a recession, the company may need cash at the exact moment many employees need cash. Liquidity therefore needs several independent sources.

---

## 13. Why public still remains an option

Private is the intended destination, not a religion.

If a mature Missionism company eventually discovers that private markets cannot provide enough capital, employee liquidity, acquisition currency, or fair financing, it can list a non-governing Capital Share class.

The contributor governance system does not need to be sold with it.

That means public markets become an escape hatch and a tool, not the definition of success.

The question is never, "Are we big enough to IPO?" The question is, "Would public liquidity advance the mission enough to justify the new pressures it creates?"

---

## 14. What we refuse to build

Missionism can become dangerous if employment turns into financial captivity. The company must not become a modern company town.

- Leaving a job should not make a normal home or car loan immediately due.
- Vested economic ownership should remain the employee's property.
- A lender should not gain mission-governance rights because an employee defaults.
- Employees should not be encouraged to borrow against ownership for endless consumption.
- The company should not guarantee unlimited share repurchases.
- Managers should not control political power simply by manipulating performance scores.
- Employees should not be able to vote themselves unlimited benefits at the expense of the company's survival.
- Outside investors should not be able to buy control of the mission.

The financial system should increase freedom outside the company, not make leaving the company harder.

## 15. The four clocks of compensation

| Time horizon | Tool | What it says |
|--------------|------|----------------|
| Today | Salary | Your work has value now. |
| This year | Bonus | You created unusual value this year. |
| Decades | Contributor ownership | You helped build something that can compound. |
| Generations | Mission governance | You earned a voice in carrying the mission forward. |

---

## 16. What success looks like

A 25-year-old joins a Missionism company.

They are paid fairly. They join a small team with a clear mission. They experiment, learn, and help create value.

Every year, some of that value becomes ownership.

Five years later, they have enough accumulated ownership to help buy their first home. A company-supported system shows them what they can safely afford, arranges conventional lenders, and makes the transaction simple.

When life breaks, they have a small low-cost buffer instead of a high-interest credit card.

As their career grows, their ownership grows. They can hold it, borrow conservatively against eligible portions, sell some during liquidity windows, or diversify some without giving up everything they built.

Twenty years later, they do not merely have a history of paychecks.

**They own assets.**

The founder can become extraordinarily wealthy too. Exceptional contributors can become extraordinarily wealthy. Outside investors can earn attractive returns. Banks can earn reasonable lending returns. None of those things are enemies of the mission.

The system simply makes one change:

> The people who spend their lives creating long-term value should steadily become owners of that value.

---

## 17. The direction

Missionism companies should be designed to remain private indefinitely.

They should build institutional-quality valuation, liquidity, lending, and diversification so private ownership becomes useful capital rather than trapped paper wealth.

They should begin founder-led and gradually become contributor-owned and contributor-governed as real value is created.

They should use outside capital when it helps the mission, but capital should buy economic participation rather than permanent control of the mission.

They should partner with existing regulated banks, mortgage lenders, insurers, and other financial institutions before trying to rebuild those industries themselves.

And they should preserve the option to list non-governing Capital Shares publicly if, someday, public markets become clearly better infrastructure for the mission.

**The goal is not to make employees dependent on the company.**

**The goal is to make their years of contribution create more independence.**

---

## 18. The promise in one paragraph

Missionism pays people for the work they do today and gives them ownership in the value they help create for tomorrow. That ownership should not sit trapped on a statement. The system should help employees turn it into real financial power: a home, a safety buffer, education, retirement, new ventures, and long-term security, while making it easier to keep ownership and let it compound. Companies should stay private when possible so they can think in decades, protect the mission, and avoid turning the daily stock price into the goal. Public markets remain available when their liquidity or capital truly advances the mission. Capital is welcome. Profit is welcome. Wealth is welcome. But the people carrying the mission should increasingly own and govern the institution they are building.
