# Mission Idea Method v0

**Status:** Proposed creative method. It creates candidate Visions and Sparks, not approved Missions or protocol rules.

## The central move

Start with a human outcome. Describe the smallest physical, biological, informational, and social parts that produce it. Identify which limits come from nature and which come from design choices. Then rebuild the system using today's capabilities, while preserving human agency and measuring the real outcome.

The generative question is:

> If we needed this human outcome but had inherited none of today's products, organizations, or routines, what system would we design?

The question opens the design space. It does not erase physics, evidence, law, culture, or the people who would live with the result.

These are different scales. A **Vision** names a durable outcome; a **Mission** organizes people and resources around a tractable part of it; a **product or protocol** supplies a mechanism; a **pilot** tests one claim. Keep all four visible so a grand idea does not masquerade as a validated plan.

## The three passes

### 1. See the world underneath the product

Record one concrete human experience: what a person is trying to do, what effort or harm recurs, who bears it, and what they value about the current arrangement. Map inputs, transformations, outputs, waste, and feedback. Include the human work that is easy to overlook: remembering, waiting, coordinating, cleaning, repairing, teaching, and deciding.

Separate assumptions into five bins:

1. **Physical or biological limit:** must be respected or measured.
2. **Human requirement:** dignity, autonomy, safety, pleasure, meaning, and accessibility.
3. **Rule or institution:** potentially changeable, but with a reason and affected parties.
4. **Historical design choice:** a candidate for redesign.
5. **Unknown:** a question for research or experiment.

For each important claim, mark **observed**, **supported**, **hypothesis**, or **unknown**. A vivid analogy is a prompt to investigate, not evidence.

### 2. Rebuild from the outcome

Write a zero-based specification in ordinary language: “A person can ___ with at most ___ human effort, at a cost of ___, while retaining ___.” Define the unacceptable costs and who might absorb them.

Generate at least three architectures by asking:

- What could be **removed** because the need beneath it can be met directly?
- What could be **standardized at the interface** so people and tools coordinate with less effort?
- What could be **moved upstream** into shared infrastructure instead of repeated in every household or organization?
- What could be **combined** so one input or process serves several needs?
- What could **regenerate or circulate** instead of being discarded and rebuilt?
- What becomes possible if the environment is designed for the cheapest sufficient tool?
- What would the system look like if the people who use, maintain, and pay for it helped set its objective?

Draw the full loop, including maintenance, failure, disposal, and learning. Name the interface that would let independent teams build compatible pieces. Keep room for personal choice and local variation.

### 3. Attack the idea before building it

Find the load-bearing hypothesis: the one claim that, if false, would make this architecture unattractive. Name the strongest existing alternative. Identify a small, reversible test that compares them on the human outcome, resource use, reliability, and distribution of benefits and burdens.

Choose a stop rule before the pilot. A pilot must say who is responsible, who may be affected, what resources it may use, what evidence counts, when it ends, and what would cause it to stop. Existing Spark and Pilot flows can hold the downstream proposal; the first-principles work should remain an upstream exploration until a claim is ready to test.

## Mission idea worksheet

Copy this for a new candidate:

```text
Observed moment:
Who experiences it, and what are they trying to accomplish?

Human outcome:
What would a better life or capability look like, in their terms?

Primitive map:
Inputs → transformations → outputs → maintenance → waste → feedback.
Where does human attention or coordination enter?

Inherited assumptions:
Which are physical limits, human requirements, rules, habits, or unknowns?

Zero-based specification:
What result, cost, effort, reliability, and freedom should the system provide?

Three different architectures:
For each: mechanism, required interface, who operates it, and new failure modes.

Mission kernel:
What durable outcome could many teams pursue without one product owning it?

Load-bearing hypothesis and best alternative:
What would make us change our mind?

First reversible test:
Who, where, how long, what resources, what measurements, what stop rule?

Evidence ledger:
Observed / supported / hypothesis / unknown; source and date for each claim.
```

## Selection standard

A promising Mission idea should make a common human outcome materially better, expose an inherited assumption, offer a mechanism beyond a slogan, permit a bounded first test, and create learning that other people can reuse. Prefer ideas that reduce repeated coordination costs without shifting hidden work onto the least powerful participants. Preserve the [Human Compact](../../spec/HUMAN_COMPACT.md): ideas and systems can be questioned radically; people keep equal standing and freedom of conscience.

Reject false precision. A score can help compare candidates, but it cannot certify desirability. A compelling Vision can remain open while an early pilot fails. The useful output of a failed pilot is a sharper world model.

## Product implication

The current Spark form begins with “What should exist?” and quickly asks for a pilot. Add an **optional upstream exploration** later: guide someone through the primitive map, inherited assumptions, three architectures, and load-bearing hypothesis. Let the person choose what becomes a Vision and which small claim becomes a Spark. Keep AI's questions and proposed architectures labeled as suggestions; never present generated feasibility, demand, or safety claims as verified. Do not turn this method into a mandatory long form for someone who already has a concrete Spark.

The next design test is to run the worksheet with people who did not create these examples. Ask whether it produces genuinely different, useful architectures and a better first experiment than the existing Spark flow. Record ideas discarded and assumptions corrected, not just attractive proposals.
