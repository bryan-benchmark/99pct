# Ownership, Capital, and the Long-Term Company

**Why Missionism companies should begin founder-led, become contributor-owned, stay private when possible, and make ownership useful in ordinary life.**

**Top sentence:**

> A paycheck pays for today. Ownership should help pay for tomorrow.

Companion (employee-facing promise): `OWNERSHIP_THAT_CHANGES_LIVES.md`. Site: `/ownership`. Spec: `spec/OWNERSHIP.md`.

---

## 1. The thesis in plain English

Most companies pay people for their time. A small group of founders and investors own most of the asset created by that work. Missionism keeps the good parts of capitalism — ownership, profit, risk-taking, markets, and investment — but changes who steadily becomes an owner.

A Missionism company can start with one founder owning nearly everything. Over decades, the people who actually build the mission earn Contributor Shares. The founder keeps the wealth already earned, but future contribution creates future ownership. Governance slowly moves from the founder toward the active contributors.

The company is designed to stay private indefinitely if private markets can give employees enough liquidity and financial usefulness. Public markets remain available later, but they are a tool, not the finish line.

---

## 2. What we are optimizing for

The goal is not to maximize stock price, employee votes, or the amount of equity given away. The goal is to build institutions that can pursue a mission for decades while making the people who create the value meaningfully wealthier and more independent.

```
mission progress × long-term behavior × useful employee ownership × capital efficiency × resilience
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
short-term pressure × governance capture × financial fragility × forced selling × complexity
```

Every design choice should be tested against that equation.

---

## 3. The ownership transition

### Founder phase

At the beginning, concentrated founder control is useful. There may be one idea, one person, little capital, and no proven organization. Democracy at this stage can create process without creating wisdom. The founder took unusual early risk and needs room to move quickly.

### Contributor phase

As the company grows, employees and other contributors earn real equity for helping create durable mission value. New Contributor Shares are minted under a precommitted system. The founder is diluted as the denominator grows; the founder does not need to hand over a fixed percentage on a calendar schedule.

### Mature mission phase

Eventually, active contributors collectively become the main governing constituency. The founder may still be very wealthy and may remain an important contributor, but permanent control does not automatically pass to heirs or to whoever can buy the most shares.

Economic ownership already earned should generally remain property. Governance can follow active contribution and constitutional rules instead of inherited wealth.

---

## 4. Equity mining: how future ownership is created

Equity mining should reward the behaviors that help a mission compound: long-term thinking, small empowered teams, patient execution, experiments, intelligent big swings, learning from failure, and measurable mission progress.

The system should work at three levels:

| Level | Question |
|-------|----------|
| **Company pool** | How much durable enterprise value and mission progress did the company create? |
| **Team allocation** | Which small teams produced meaningful outcomes? |
| **Individual allocation** | Who made unusually important contributions inside those teams? |

Major mission milestones can unlock larger pools. Ordinary years can unlock smaller pools. The formula should be predictable enough to trust but not so mechanical that employees learn to game a points system.

The founder can also continue earning Contributor Shares later in life by continuing to contribute. **Missionism is not anti-founder. It is anti-permanent entitlement to future value that other people create.**

MCU accounting (`spec/MCU_PROTOCOL.md`) records contribution. Contributor Shares are the long-term economic mapping. Never hard-code `1 MCU = X shares`.

---

## 5. Separate the jobs that one share normally performs

Traditional stock often bundles three things together: economic ownership, governance, and liquidity. Missionism works better if those are separated.

| Layer | What it does | Who gets it |
|-------|----------------|-------------|
| **Contributor Shares** | Economic ownership + contributor governance | Earned through contribution |
| **Capital Shares** | Economic ownership with limited or no ordinary mission governance | Bought by outside investors or created when contributors seek external liquidity |
| **Mission Constitution / purpose layer** | Protects the mission and the basic ownership rules | Cannot be purchased like ordinary stock |

A core rule is **one-way conversion**: Contributor Shares may be converted into Capital Shares when a holder wants outside liquidity. Capital Shares do not convert back into Contributor governance.

> Capital can buy economics. It cannot simply buy the mission.

### Why would an outside investor buy Capital Shares without mission control?

Because most investors are buying an economic asset, not a meaningful personal vote. They care about whether the company can grow, produce cash, distribute value, and eventually give them a liquid way to exit. A tiny shareholder vote in a giant company rarely gives an ordinary investor practical control anyway.

But non-governing capital cannot mean unprotected capital. Outside investors still need clear legal economic rights: honest reporting, protection against self-dealing, rules for dilution, treatment in a sale or liquidation, and limits on changing the economics of their class.

The trade-off is real: some investors may demand a lower price or higher expected return because they cannot replace management or redirect the mission. Missionism should accept some **governance discount** if that is the cost of keeping capital from purchasing the constitution. The test is whether that discount is smaller than the long-term value of protected mission control.

## 6. What existing companies already prove

Missionism is new as a complete system, but many pieces already exist.

| Example | What it proves | What it does not solve |
|---------|----------------|------------------------|
| **Publix** | A very large private company can make employee ownership central to culture and compensation. Publix remains privately held and says eligible associates can earn or buy stock. | It does not provide a universal, highly liquid public market or the Missionism contribution-governance system. |
| **Stripe** | A giant private company can create employee liquidity through organized tender offers without being public. | Tender windows are still less continuous and automatic than a public exchange. |
| **UPS** | Different share classes can separate voting power from public liquidity. UPS Class A has more votes and is primarily held by employees/retirees and related holders; Class B trades publicly. | Its structure is not contributor democracy and does not implement Missionism equity mining. |
| **Public dual-class companies** | Markets already tolerate the idea that economic ownership and voting power can differ. | Founder control can become permanent or dynastic if the transition is badly designed. |

Missionism is therefore not inventing employee ownership, private liquidity, dual-class stock, or long-term capital from nothing. The new work is combining them into one constitutional system.

## 7. The private-versus-public decision

This is the biggest strategic choice. Both models can work. They optimize for different things.

### The strongest case for going public

- **Liquidity:** employees can sell shares quickly instead of waiting for a tender window.
- **Price discovery:** the market continuously shows what investors are willing to pay.
- **Collateral:** lenders understand publicly traded securities more easily than unusual private shares.
- **Diversification:** employees can sell or diversify small portions whenever rules permit.
- **Capital access:** a public company can raise very large amounts of equity and debt from broad markets.
- **Acquisition currency:** public shares can be used to buy other companies.
- **Infrastructure already exists:** brokers, custodians, exchanges, analysts, lenders, and securities systems already know how to work with public stock.

Going public does not make the operating business more profitable by itself. It does not magically increase EBITDA. What it can change is the price investors are willing to pay for the same earnings because the shares are liquid, easy to trade, and supported by deep capital markets. That can lower the cost of capital or increase the valuation multiple. It can also go the other way: public-company costs, weak growth, or disliked governance can reduce value.

### The real costs of going public

- A second scoreboard appears: the stock price changes every day, even when the mission did not.
- Employees may experience market movements as personal wins and losses, creating short-term emotional pressure.
- Leadership spends attention on analysts, guidance, filings, investor relations, activists, and headlines.
- Competitors learn more because public reporting is extensive.
- Public-stock borrowing can become dangerous if lenders mark collateral to market and demand more collateral after a sudden decline.
- Unusual governance can be criticized or discounted by investors even when it is legal and intentional.
- The organization can slowly optimize for the market narrative instead of the mission.

### The strongest case for staying private

- The company can operate on a slower clock: quarters and years instead of seconds.
- The mission can remain the main scoreboard.
- Founders and contributors can experiment with governance without explaining every change to public markets.
- The organization can make long-duration investments that may look bad for several quarters.
- The culture can preserve a simple identity: we own this company together.
- The company can choose its investors and favor patient capital.
- Private status can reduce public scrutiny and the organizational attention consumed by market noise.

### The real costs of staying private

- Liquidity has to be created deliberately.
- A credible share value must be calculated periodically rather than discovered continuously on an exchange.
- Lenders need standardized information and legal rights before they will treat private shares as useful collateral.
- Large employee liquidity requests can arrive at the same time during a recession.
- The company must avoid promising unlimited repurchases that could drain operating cash.
- Very large acquisitions or capital raises may be harder or more expensive than they would be in public markets.
- Private-market infrastructure becomes a real operating system that must be designed, audited, and trusted.

## 8. Our decision: private is the destination; public is the escape hatch

Missionism companies should be designed to remain private indefinitely.

That is different from saying they must never go public. The constitution should preserve the ability to list Capital Shares later if public markets become clearly better infrastructure for the mission.

The default question should not be, "When are we big enough to IPO?" It should be, "What problem would an IPO solve that we cannot solve privately at a reasonable cost?"

**Never treat IPO as graduation.**

Public listing becomes justified only when one or more of these become material:

- Employee liquidity is persistently inadequate despite mature private liquidity programs.
- The cost of private capital is materially higher than public capital.
- The company needs acquisition currency at a scale private markets cannot efficiently provide.
- Employee diversification and collateralization remain too difficult privately.
- The private system creates more complexity or fragility than a public listing would create.

Until then, staying private protects the scarce resource Missionism cares about most: **organizational attention directed toward the mission.**

### Staying private does not mean starving the company of capital

A private Missionism company still has several ways to fund growth. Public stock is only one financing tool.

| Capital source | What the company gives up | Best use |
|----------------|---------------------------|----------|
| **Retained profits** | No ownership; cash that could have been distributed | Best when the company already produces strong cash flow. |
| **Private equity / Capital Shares** | Economic ownership and an expected return | Best for risky expansion when repayment cannot be promised. |
| **Bank debt / private credit** | Interest, repayment, and lender covenants | Best when cash flows are predictable enough to service debt. |
| **Bonds** | Interest and eventual repayment | Useful for very large, mature borrowers; being publicly traded is not required in principle to use debt markets, though access and pricing vary. |
| **Public equity** | Economic ownership plus public-market obligations | Best when the liquidity and scale of public capital are worth the attention and governance costs. |

Debt and equity solve different problems. Equity lets investors absorb risk but dilutes economics. Debt preserves ownership but must be repaid. Retained earnings are often the cheapest capital of all once the business is a reliable cash generator. Missionism should choose the cheapest form that does not distort the mission.

## 9. The private financial system we must build

If we choose private as the intended destination, we must recreate the useful parts of public markets without recreating the entire public-market culture.

1. **Recurring independent valuation.** Publish a rigorous reference value on a predictable schedule, such as quarterly.
2. **Contributor and Capital share classes.** Keep contributor governance separate from outside investment.
3. **One-way conversion.** Let contributors turn some ownership into non-governing Capital Shares when they want external liquidity.
4. **Organized liquidity windows.** Let employees sell reasonable amounts at scheduled times instead of promising unlimited daily liquidity.
5. **Multiple buyers.** Allow employees, the company within limits, long-term institutions, and diversified Missionism funds to provide liquidity.
6. **Standardized lending data.** Give approved lenders a consistent package: valuation, vesting status, transfer rules, financial statements, historical liquidity, and collateral rights.
7. **Employee diversification.** Let people reduce concentration without abandoning the Missionism ecosystem.
8. **Hard liquidity limits.** The operating company never promises to buy every share whenever employees want cash.

## 10. Ownership becomes an operating system for ordinary life

The point of employee ownership is not to make a number on a cap table larger. The point is to let years of contribution create more financial freedom.

The employee experience should eventually be simple:

> You have $500,000 of vested ownership. Here is how much is eligible for home financing support. Here is a safe house budget based on your salary and debts. Here is what your paycheck will look like after the mortgage. Here are approved lenders and a salaried home advocate.

Missionism should organize the complexity behind the scenes and show the employee the decision in plain language.

---

## 11. Housing: the clearest example

Suppose Maya has worked at a Missionism company for five years and accumulated $500,000 of vested Contributor Shares.

Instead of forcing her to sell $100,000 of ownership for a down payment, an approved lending program may recognize a conservative portion of her ownership as financial support. A simple starting concept could be a 5:1 collateral relationship: about $5 of eligible share value for each $1 of supported financing. The final advance rate should be set by professional credit analysis and regulation, not by philosophy.

Maya still gets a conventional 30-year mortgage and still has to afford the monthly payment. Missionism helps with the hardest parts: understanding what she can safely afford, arranging lenders, creating down-payment support, and reducing transaction friction.

A salaried or flat-fee Home Advocate can help her choose a home without being paid more for pushing her toward a more expensive transaction.

**Five years of contribution → real ownership → real home-buying power.**

The company did not give Maya a free house. Her work created an asset that helped her buy one.

---

## 12. The $10,000 Life Buffer

A smaller benefit may be even more useful day to day. Eligible employees could have access to a simple emergency buffer, perhaps up to $10,000 depending on income, vested ownership, and applicable lending rules.

A broken HVAC system, childcare gap, emergency move, or car repair should not force a long-term owner to sell shares or use a high-interest credit card.

Repayment could occur over time through authorized payroll deduction or another ordinary payment method. Any zero-interest or subsidized structure needs proper tax, wage-deduction, consumer-lending, and state-law design.

The aim is not to make money from employee emergencies. It is to make it easier for employees to remain long-term owners.

**Product note:** Lending against ownership is Phase 7 / explicitly not yet. See `BUILD_PLAN.md`. This document is doctrine, not a shipping checklist.

## 13. Other ways ownership can improve compensation

| Life need | Missionism role | Design principle |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| Home | Mortgage partners, down-payment financing, home advocates | Use ownership to help acquire a durable asset. |
| Emergency | Small low-cost buffer | Protect ownership from short-term shocks. |
| Education | Financing or subsidy for useful training | Invest in human capital. |
| Car | Negotiated pricing, financing, clear paycheck impact | Be more conservative with depreciating assets. |
| Relocation | Up-front moving capital repaid over time | Remove cash-flow friction. |
| Insurance | Group purchasing and transparent comparison | Increase purchasing power without simply increasing wages. |
| New mission company | Potential financing against a small, conservative portion of accumulated ownership | Let one mission seed another. |
| Retirement | Liquidity, diversification, distributions, and planning | Turn decades of contribution into durable independence. |

Missionism can also use group scale to negotiate mortgages, insurance, cars, tax help, legal help, and other services. The goal is not a coupon book. The goal is to make one dollar of compensation create more lifetime value.

## 14. Compensation should work on four clocks

| Clock | Tool | What it rewards |
|-------|------|-----------------|
| Today | Salary | The value of current labor and a normal life today. |
| This year | Bonus | Unusually strong short-term contribution. |
| Decades | Contributor Shares | Long-term value creation and compounding. |
| Generations | Mission governance | A voice in carrying the mission forward. |

This is better than asking one instrument — salary or stock — to do everything.

---

## 15. Do not turn Missionism into a company town

The system becomes dangerous if the employer controls too much of an employee's financial life. Missionism must increase independence, not dependency.

- Leaving the company should not make an ordinary home, education, or car loan immediately due.
- Vested economic ownership remains the employee's property subject to the governing terms.
- A lender should never gain contributor governance rights because an employee defaults.
- The operating company should partner with regulated banks, mortgage lenders, insurers, and servicers before trying to own those industries.
- Employees should be free to use outside lenders and advisers.
- Financial data use should be limited, consent-based, and separated from ordinary employment decisions.
- The system should not encourage people to borrow against shares for endless consumption.
- The safest products should acquire or protect durable assets: homes, education, emergency stability, and productive ventures.

---

## 16. The recession test

The private system is only good if it survives bad years. The biggest danger is not one employee default. It is many things going wrong at the same time.

Imagine a recession: company value falls 60%, some employees lose jobs, thousands want cash, lenders become cautious, and the company itself needs to preserve cash.

A naive system could create a run: employees sell → liquidity dries up → valuation falls → lenders demand more collateral → more employees sell.

Missionism should design against that loop from the beginning:

- Use conservative starting collateral values and advance rates.
- Avoid ordinary mark-to-market margin loans for core life needs.
- Prefer fixed-term or amortizing credit that does not automatically become due because the share value fell this quarter.
- Use several sources of liquidity instead of making the employer the buyer of last resort.
- Cap company repurchases as a percentage of available cash or free cash flow.
- Stress-test the system against 50–70% valuation declines and simultaneous employee demand.
- Diversify lender and investor exposure across many Missionism companies as the ecosystem grows.

---

## 17. The public-market recession test

Public stock solves liquidity but creates a different kind of fragility. In a recession, a public share price can fall 40% or 60% very quickly. A lender using normal securities-backed credit may respond by reducing borrowing capacity or demanding additional collateral.

That means public ownership is not automatically safer for employee borrowing. It is easier to price and sell, but it is also continuously marked to market. Missionism would still need conservative lending rules even if Capital Shares were publicly traded.

This is another reason private periodic valuation may be a feature rather than a defect: it can reduce reflexive daily pressure while still providing enough information for disciplined underwriting.

---

## 18. The other stress tests we must solve

| Stress | Question the constitution must answer |
|--------|---------------------------------------|
| Founder dies early | Who holds temporary authority and how does the planned contributor transition continue? |
| Founder becomes ineffective | How can contributors or independent governance remove management without destroying the mission? |
| Top team games equity mining | How are allocations audited and appealed? |
| Employees vote themselves excessive benefits | What financial guardrails sit above ordinary votes? |
| Mission advances but profits fall | How long can the system fund mission work before the economics become unsustainable? |
| Profits soar but mission stalls | Can the system reward economic success without mistaking it for mission success? |
| Employees need mass liquidity | Which buyers and funds absorb shares without draining the operating company? |
| Private capital becomes expensive | At what measurable point does public listing become the cheaper tool? |
| Retirees hold huge economic stakes | How do economics remain theirs while active governance stays with active contributors? |
| Company has fraud or scandal | What independent oversight protects employees and outside capital? |

## 19. What we build ourselves and what we should not

### Missionism should build

- The contribution and equity-mining rules.
- The founder-to-contributor governance transition.
- Contributor and Capital share architecture.
- The Mission Constitution and protected mission rules.
- The recurring private valuation and liquidity standard.
- The employee ownership dashboard that makes future wealth visible.
- The rules for when ownership may support financing.
- The network of approved financial partners and transparent employee advocates.

### Missionism should initially partner for

- 30-year mortgage origination and servicing.
- Consumer lending and regulatory compliance.
- Bank custody and payments.
- Insurance underwriting.
- Brokerage / private secondary-market infrastructure.
- Independent valuation and audits.
- Real-estate closing and other licensed transaction services.

AI can make these workflows much simpler. It does not remove the need for regulated institutions, capital, licenses, fair-lending controls, or professional risk management.

## 20. Why this can be a better compensation package

A company can spend the same total amount on an employee and create very different lifetime outcomes.

Traditional package: salary + scattered benefits. Missionism package: competitive salary + annual upside + automatic ownership + tools that help preserve and use that ownership.

The important measure is not just annual cash compensation. It is **lifetime employee net worth, security, agency, and freedom per dollar the employer spends.**

One extra dollar of salary is valuable once. One dollar of ownership may compound for decades. A lending and purchasing system can make that ownership useful before retirement without forcing the employee to sell all of it.

## 21. What success looks like

A 25-year-old joins a Missionism company. They are paid fairly and join a small team with a clear mission. They try things, learn, and create value.

Every year, some of that durable value becomes ownership.

Five years later, their accumulated ownership may help them buy their first home. They can see exactly what they can afford and what their paycheck will look like afterward. When life breaks, a small low-cost buffer may keep them away from high-interest debt and prevent a forced sale of long-term shares.

As their career grows, their ownership grows. They can hold it, use carefully limited portions to support financing, sell some during liquidity windows, or diversify some without abandoning everything they built.

Twenty years later, they do not merely have a history of paychecks.

**They own assets.**

The founder may also be extraordinarily wealthy. Exceptional contributors may become extraordinarily wealthy. Outside investors may earn excellent returns. Banks may earn reasonable lending returns. Profit and wealth are not enemies of Missionism.

> The people who spend their lives creating long-term value should steadily become owners of that value.

## 22. The pathway we are choosing

1. Start founder-led and private.
2. Write the Mission Constitution and contributor-transition rules before the company is large enough for those rules to threaten anyone.
3. Build equity mining slowly and test it for gaming, fairness, and mission alignment.
4. Create Contributor Shares and a separate Capital Share pathway for outside money and future liquidity.
5. Build recurring private valuation and scheduled liquidity.
6. Partner with banks and lenders so vested ownership becomes useful for homes, emergency buffers, education, and other high-value life needs.
7. Build diversification and external private capital as the company matures.
8. Stay private indefinitely if the system continues to provide enough capital and liquidity.
9. List Capital Shares publicly only if public markets become a clearly better tool for the mission. Never treat IPO as graduation.

## 23. Why the other directions are worse

| Alternative | Why it is tempting | Why we reject it as the default |
|-------------|--------------------|---------------------------------|
| Traditional founder capitalism | Fast, simple, familiar | Future ownership can remain concentrated even after thousands of people create most of the value; governance can become dynastic. |
| Immediate employee democracy | Feels fair from day one | Too much governance before there is a mature institution; can slow early experimentation and blur accountability. |
| Fixed founder giveaway by time | Simple schedule | Rewards waiting rather than contribution and can transfer ownership even when little value was created. |
| IPO as the goal | Maximum liquidity and familiar capital markets | Adds continuous market pressure and can make price a rival to mission before it is necessary. |
| Company promises unlimited buybacks | Makes private stock feel liquid | Can create a liquidity crisis exactly when the company and employees are both under stress. |
| Generic margin lending against shares | Very easy to understand | Can trigger forced sales after valuation drops and creates correlated risk between job, stock, and debt. |
| Pay everything in cash | Simple and liquid | Employees do not build ownership in the asset they are helping create; long-term incentives weaken. |
| Replace salary with equity | Maximizes ownership | Pushes too much company risk onto workers and makes ordinary life harder. |
| Employer owns every financial service | Maximum integration | Creates regulatory complexity and the danger of financial captivity. Partner first; integrate only when scale proves it is better. |

## 24. The one-paragraph explanation to the world

Missionism companies pay people fairly for the work they do today and give them ownership in the long-term value they help create. The company can begin with a founder owning nearly everything, but over decades new Contributor Shares move ownership and governance toward the people carrying the mission. We intend these companies to stay private because a private company can think in years and decades instead of watching a stock price every second. But private ownership should not mean trapped paper wealth. Missionism therefore builds recurring valuation, organized liquidity, outside non-governing capital, and conservative financial tools that can help employees use their ownership for real life — especially homes, emergency stability, education, and retirement — without forcing them to sell everything they have built. If public markets someday become clearly better infrastructure for the mission, Capital Shares can be listed without selling the mission itself. The goal is not to make workers dependent on their employer. The goal is for years of contribution to create more independence.

## 25. Reference examples informing the design

These examples do not equal Missionism; they show that important pieces of the architecture already exist.

- **Publix:** private employee ownership and periodic private stock valuation. Publix says it is the largest employee-owned company in the United States and that eligible associates can earn or purchase privately held stock.
- **Stripe:** private employee liquidity through recurring tender offers. In February 2026 Stripe announced a tender offer for current and former employees at a $159 billion valuation.
- **UPS:** dual-class structure separating voting power and public liquidity. UPS states that Class A shares have 10 votes per share and are primarily held by employees, retirees, founder descendants and related trusts; Class B has one vote per share and trades publicly on the NYSE.
- **Delaware corporate law:** flexible share classes and voting structures can support different economic and governance rights, subject to the charter, securities law, tax law, and other legal requirements.

*Source notes: Publix corporate/jobs materials (2026); Stripe newsroom, Feb. 24, 2026; UPS 2026 SEC filings; Delaware General Corporation Law. This document presents a design thesis, not legal, tax, securities, lending, or mortgage advice.*
